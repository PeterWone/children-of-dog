# Tell us a story

A long time ago in a continent far away, the people with the weapons were concerned that the people doing all the work might take advantage of all that privacy to make up their own minds about what needed doing.

What with the continent being very large, a lot of the inhabitants couldn't access important services like taxation, policing and the wholesale indoctrination known in these latter days as schooling. Worse, the land was vast; it could be _years_ between opportunities to tell them what they wanted. 

As you can imagine, this is not something one can allow to stand; if you leave people alone for long enough they start to think for themselves, and this can only lead to trouble.

The leaders of that land were very concerned. Some of their charges didn't see how right and proper it was to submit to authority. Others went so far as to say that sending _all_ the gold and wheat to a faraway king was a dumb idea and they didn't want to do it. But fear not, it all worked out in the end: the leaders pretended to be useless aristocrats while all the troublemakers gathered in one place, and then they shot some and arrested the rest.

Things calmed down after this display of managerial prowess. The locals might have been too common for a proper appreciation of the benefits of civilisation but they did seem to understand a good flogging if it were administered consistently and fairly, ie to everyone who didn't say "How high?" when the governor said jump.

The faraway king's administrators sagely noted that order had been restored, and things placed in their proper perspective for the locals. On their advice he magnanimously allowed the people to choose which of several pre-approved sycophants would tell them what they wanted.

"Hey uncle Ken," said a suspiciously deep male voice, "Why are they called 'public servants' when they give all the orders?"

A much younger voice piped up. "That's not _my_ experience of schooling. Ms Carter likes questions. She says we're turning into a pack of uncontrollable reprobates and she couldn't be prouder."

"It's still indoctrination," said Ken. "We're teaching you to see the world the way we see it. But our way of looking at it starts with your right and responsibility to call bullshit when you hear it. Now, where was I? Oh yes."

This proved enormously popular. People felt heard. They felt that they had agency. They felt, above all, that if things went wrong it was their own fault for voting incorrectly — averting a great many awkward questions about who actually made decisions and why.

The candidates were chosen by parties and funded by donors. By happy chance these were exactly the same sort of people as before, or their children, or the people who had bought what their children sold. The faraway king was replaced by a board of directors, which is much the same with better lawyers.

The schooling, remember the schooling? It's a song about schooling.

Shorty leaned in, "I think Arlo might be a little before their time, Ken."

Ken continued, as if nothing had happened, a smile upon his lips.

The schooling was by now thoroughly established. It turned out that twelve years of practice at sitting still, following instructions, and producing the correct answer on demand was excellent preparation for a lifetime of voting for whoever seemed most likely to leave things as they were.

Then propaganda was invented, which considerably lightened the burden of governance. The sycophants' responsibilities were extended to a vital new function: keeping an ear to the ground for confusion about what people were supposed to want. When large numbers of people held opinions not issued through proper channels, it fell to the sycophants to explain that what they actually wanted was precisely what was already being done on their behalf. When this was too much of a stretch, they explained instead that troublemakers had made a complete hash of things, and that the only way out was to trust the right people to sort it out. Curiously, this often entailed new government powers.

This worked splendidly, provided you were the king or one of his representatives, and Australia rode to prosperity on the sheep's back with periodic opportunities to die nobly for the mother country and get your name on a tiny little plaque in a forgotten field.

"Uncle Ken," asked Aiden, "what's the difference between indoctrination and propaganda? And yes that's a school question. They're not the same but it's, I can't put it in words."

Ken looked thoughtful. "Forest?"

"Why bless mah soul, Kenneth, are you asking me to opine?"

"It's a one-time thing. Ten, nine, eight-"

"Indoc is the organised imposition of a coherent and fairly complete world-view. This may be to inhibit critical thinking or it may be to reduce cognitive load under stress. Our combat protocols are indoc. You have to _know_ without discussion what other team members will do and how they will react. This is a good example of indoc that's as closely aligned with reality as we can get it. Propaganda is more limited in scope and generally concerned with shaping opinion. It deals primarily with framing and perception. Like convincing people to want what the government is doing. Effective propaganda is usually the truth. It's just not the whole truth and it's rarely nothing but the truth. Generally it exploits peoples' cherished beliefs to get acceptance without scrutiny."

He turned to Aiden.

"That is why Ms Carter, and Ken, and me for that matter, that's why we bang on about challenging the things you're told. We don't mean 'be an argumentative prick'-"

"That's Forest's job."

"Yes, thank you Ken. But making people back up their claims, or at least explain why they think so, that's important. Even if they aren't trying to deceive you or manipulate you, they might be wrong. I cannot count the times that someone's said 'how do you know that?' and the answer was 'um, er, I suppose I don't really, not for certain.' I make a lot of guesses. I'm good at it, but they're still guesses. The most important thing the ground teams ever learnt from me is 'not everything he says has been verified'."

"Why don't you check?"

"I'm lazy, and sometimes there isn't time. In combat checked intel is gold, but an experienced guess is silver."

Ken snorted. "How long have you been waiting to trot that one out?"

"Yesterday arvo. Like it?"

"It's not total horseshit."

"Hey Ken, next weekend is Ordnance Day. Can me and Scott come?"

Ken stared levelly at him. "Can you?"

"No. Mum said Lisa earned her privileges."

"Right. Is there anything you can do about that?"

Aiden looked downcast. "Not by the weekend."

"Why don't you go fishing with Scott? Or you could put in some time on the range."
