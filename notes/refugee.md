# **FULL SCENE (ALL INTEGRATED)**

The refugee kid had just finished explaining that in the city, people hid things because there "wasn't enough."  
The valley kids froze — not offended, not angry, just stunned.

Riss asked, very slowly:

> "Not enough *what*?"

"Food. Clothes. Tools. Stuff," the refugee said.

The kids stared at him.

A boy blurted:

> "But we saw your city!"

Another jumped in:

> "There were *mountains* of things!"

A girl spread her arms wide.

> "Whole buildings full of clothes! Piles of metal! Boxes and boxes of tools!  
> Most of it wasn't very well made — it was all pretty flimsy, and it looked like it was made that way on purpose."

Riss added, baffled:

> "You could fill ten villages with the stuff just lying on the ground."

The refugee looked embarrassed.

> "Yeah, but… people couldn't get it."

The kids stared at him like he'd said the ocean sometimes turned into sand.

A boy asked:

> "Why not? It was right there."

"It belonged to someone," the refugee said.

The kids recoiled slightly — not in fear, but in the way you'd react if someone said water sometimes burns.

Riss tried to make sense of it.

> "So… people were scared of not having enough… even when they were standing on top of giant piles of stuff?"

He nodded.

A boy shook his head.

> "That's like going to a feast and piling three plates high and running off because you're worried about being hungry."

A girl snorted.

> "If people behaved like that, there's a fair chance the latecomers might actually go a bit hungry."

Riss added, blunt as a dropped hammer:

> "That's just **stupid**. Someone would tell them, and they'd find you, and make you eat **all of it**."

The refugee looked horrified.

A younger boy, trying to be fair, said:

> "You might take more than you need if you're going to build something and you don't want to be coming back for more — there might be rain coming or something."

Riss nodded.

> "Yeah, that's different. That's planning. But taking extra just because you're scared? That's dumb."

The refugee swallowed.

> "That's why you need accountants. To keep track. So people don't take too much."

Riss frowned.

> "But if someone takes too much, you just tell them. And they fix it."

A boy added:

> "You don't need a special person to write it down. You just… deal with it."

"But there were too many people," the refugee said. "You couldn't see what everyone was doing."

The kids exchanged glances.

A girl said:

> "If you can't see what people are doing, your group is too big."

Riss nodded.

> "And if your group is too big, you don't fix it with a score‑keeper. You fix the group."

The teacher reached into the box and set a **metal badge** on the table.

"That's for police," the refugee said.

The kids leaned in.

Riss picked it up.

> "What do they do?"

"They tell people what to do. And stop people doing bad things."

A boy frowned.

> "Why would you need a special person for that?"

"Because people don't always listen," the refugee said.

"To who?" Riss asked.

"The rules."

The kids stared at him.

A girl said:

> "If someone's doing something dangerous, you tell them. If they don't stop, you get an adult. If they still don't stop, everyone knows and they get told harder."

A boy added:

> "And if they *still* don't stop, they don't stay."

Riss set the badge down.

> "You don't need a badge for that. You just need eyes and a mouth."

Next came the **tie**.

Riss held it up like it might bite.

> "So this is for someone who talks instead of doing?"

The refugee nodded.

"Politicians. They talk to people. They decide things."

A boy asked:

> "If all they do is talk, how would they know what's needed?"

"They ask people," the refugee said.

Riss frowned.

> "Why can't people ask each other?"

The refugee hesitated.

"They decide what's best for everyone."

Riss recoiled.

> "I don't think I'd like someone telling me what I should want."

A boy nodded.

> "Even if they were right, that would piss me off."

Riss dropped the tie back in the box.

> "We don't have that job."

Then came the **flag**.

"Diplomats use them," the refugee said. "They talk to other countries."

"Why do you need a special person to talk?" a boy asked.

"Because the groups don't get along."

"If you don't get along, you fix the problem," a girl said.

"Diplomats stop wars," the refugee insisted.

A boy said:

> "If you need a special person to stop a fight, your group is broken."

"No," the refugee said. "They're all acting like fools because they don't think there will be enough — which there won't, *because* they're acting like fools."

The kids stared.

He continued:

> "They're always growing. Population, industry, everything. Resources are limited and they all want each other's stuff."

Riss blinked.

> "So why don't they just stop growing? The world isn't getting bigger. Obviously growing is a bad idea."

Before the refugee could answer, a younger boy jumped in:

> "That's crazy. If everyone here had three children, do you think the creek would get bigger to suit?"

The refugee blinked.

The boy continued:

> "It wouldn't. The creek stays the same size. So we don't have three children each. That's just… obvious."

A girl nodded:

> "And the smith's duty isn't effort. Or making a mountain of stuff. His duty is to meet needs as best he can."

Riss added:

> "Yeah. Not ‘just enough for right now'. They do a production run, warehouse the surplus, and then retool for something else that'll be needed."

Another kid chimed in:

> "They keep doing that until they're a season ahead on everything."

A boy grinned:

> "And then they switch to repair clinic."

The refugee frowned.

> "Repair clinic?"

Riss nodded.

> "Yeah. People bring in broken stuff. And the smiths teach them how to fix it. You learn by repairing under supervision."

A girl added:

> "It's fun. And it means the smiths don't have to waste time fixing every little thing."

Another kid:

> "And it means people don't throw things away like idiots."

Riss shrugged:

> "So the smith meets needs, stays ahead, and then teaches. That's the job."

The refugee stared — because in his world, "more" was always the goal, "enough" was suspicious, and "teaching people to repair things" was something companies actively tried to prevent.

The teacher finally spoke.

> "Is that what you observed in Adelaide —  
> **more for everyone**?"

The refugee went still.

Riss said softly:

> "It didn't look like more for everyone."

A boy added:

> "It looked like people fighting over scraps in a place full of stuff."

The refugee's shoulders tightened — defensive, overwhelmed, cornered.

The valley kids leaned in, curious, a little too sharp.

The teacher clapped once.

> "Alright. Enough."

The kids froze.

She stepped between them.

> "I'd like to remind you all that our new friend did **not** single‑handedly create all those problems."

The kids settled.

> "He's here because his parents didn't want to be a part of it," she said.  
> "And he's learning why things are different here."

Riss nodded.

> "We're not mad at him. We're mad at the world that made him need all those weird jobs."

The refugee exhaled.

For the first time, he realised the teacher wasn't protecting him from the kids.  
She was protecting the kids from becoming something they didn't want to be.

# **CONTINUATION 1: The Workshop Tour**

The teacher closed the box.

> "Alright. Let's show him how things actually work."

The kids brightened instantly — field trip energy.

They led the refugee down the path to the smithy.  
The building wasn't big, but it *felt* big — open walls, racks of tools, bins of sorted scrap, the smell of oil and hot metal.

A girl pointed at a row of machines:

> "Those are the rollers. That's the punch. That's the bender. They built those themselves."

A boy added:

> "And that's the new rail‑track roller. He's still tuning it."

The refugee stared.  
These weren't factories.  
They were *tools for people*, not people for tools.

---

# **CONTINUATION 2: Learning to fly**

The smith — a broad‑shouldered woman with soot on her face — waved them in.

> "You the new kid? Come here."

She handed him a broken hinge.

> "See what you can do with this."

He stared at it.

> "It's broken."

"That's why it's back here in the smithy."

He was a study in confusion so she rescued him.

"Where do you think these things come from?"

"Tradesmen have them in their cars? I guess they get them from somewhere...a shop?"

"Sure, but where does the shop get it?"

"They order them." He was more certain of this.

"And where does the warehouse get them?"

He looked frustrated, cornered. "I don't know. Probably there's some sort of machine that makes them."

"From where you're from that may be so. Here, I make them. Or my prentices do, and I clean up their mess and give them an earful until it's something to be proud of."

> "You'll learn."

She guided his hands, showed him the heat, the quench, the alignment jig.  
He felt the metal soften and yield, obedient to the touch of a master. She spoke of crystal lattices and eutectic curves, and the use of sudden cooling to harden things.

For the first time in his life, he made something *work*.

Across the years a young man in a lumber jacket changed the world for his friend and a fire began to smoulder.

His breath caught.

The smith nodded.

> "See? Not hard. Just takes care."

The refugee looked at the hinge like it was magic.

---

# **CONTINUATION 3: The Teacher's Quiet Explanation**

Later, walking back, the teacher spoke softly so only he could hear.

> "We don't have those old jobs because we don't have the problems that made them necessary."

He looked up at her.

> "Why not?"

She smiled — not kindly, but truthfully.

> "Because we built a world where people don't have to act like fools."

She let that settle.

> "And now you're part of it."

He didn't know what to do with any of this. The things the valley kids said kept knocking holes in the things he'd brought with him, and every time he tried to patch one up, another one tore open somewhere else. It felt like trying to hold a bucket of water with cracks running through it. He kept tightening his grip and losing more anyway.

He wanted the Adelaide version of the world to make sense, because it always had before, but it wasn't behaving. Every time he reached for it, it slid away from him, like trying to grab something underwater. The valley kids weren't acting like the villains he'd been told about. They weren't acting like anything he recognised. They were too calm, too settled, too… normal. And that made everything worse, because it left him with nothing solid to push against.

He tried to tell himself they were wrong, or lying, or missing something important, but the arguments wouldn't form properly. They kept collapsing halfway through, turning into loose thoughts that didn't connect to anything. He could feel the shape of the story he'd grown up with, but not the weight of it. It was like remembering a dream that used to feel real and now just feels embarrassing.

Something in his stomach kept dropping, the way it does when you miss a step in the dark. He didn't want to think about what that meant. He didn't want to think about anything, really. The objects on the table weren't helping. They looked like pieces of a machine he'd been told was essential, except now he couldn't remember what any of it was supposed to do, or why it mattered, or why anyone had cared so much.

He stayed quiet because opening his mouth felt dangerous. He didn't trust what might come out. He didn't trust himself to sound like someone who understood his own world, let alone this one. Everything felt loose. Unreliable. Like the inside of his head had been shaken and nothing had settled back where it belonged.

The world was cut loose, flotsam on waves, crashing against him endlessly. He was going under.

A retired spy watched his turmoil sympathetically, and decided it was time for a visit to the smithy.
