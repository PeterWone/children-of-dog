# Politically Correct

KM: "That is _not_ what I meant. It's not even what I said!" <br>
FG: "That's what happens when you let people centralise."<br>
— The very unhappy ghost of Karl Marx talking to Forest Grump

---

Rebecca Morrison felt a bit like she'd missed a meeting. Not a literal meeting. These people didn't _do_ meetings. They socialised, and there was quite a bit of organising, and people did take notes, because there was a lot going on. But no capital M meetings. 

People helped each other as a matter of course, then dissolved back into their own days. They kept tabs, because some things take time, and other things can't wait. What they didn't do was send invoices. And when she'd offered to help with the community garden, Mrs. Patterson had stopped her when she ws leaving, and asked why she wasn't taking anything. Did her children have allergies or something like that? When she said it wasn't hers to take the old lady laughed and told her to stop being silly and take whatever her family needed, as though this were obvious and normal.

She mentioned it cautiously to Des that evening. He'd shrugged and said he was starting to like it. She said nothing and watched.

---

The Hendersons' kitchen table groaned under scones and jam still warm from the morning's berry picking. Around it sat four women. Janet Henderson, Sarah Webb, Maria Santos — whose husband had just started in the foundry, and Rebecca. Sarah was a psych graduate. She had, it seemed, given up on HR to teach the valley children.

Rebecca set her cup down. "My grandfather fled Hungary in '56. My father drilled into me that communism destroys everything it touches." She looked around the table. "I see what's happening here, and I can't think of a polite way to say what it looks like."

Nobody looked offended. Maria spread jam on a scone. "Go on, then."

"Collectivisation. Shared resources. Decisions made by the group instead of individuals. It's textbook."

Janet looked puzzled. "What decisions? Mrs. Patterson saw the Williams family needed preserves and cleared a bit of space in her pantry. She _likes_ them."

"Forest would flip out if anyone tried to set up a distribution committee," Maria said. "The moment you create bureaucracy to manage sharing, you've killed the sharing and kept the bureaucracy."

"But the Stalinists&mdash;"

"Were raging centralists at huge scale," Sarah said, "and they weren't doing it to their own children."

Rebecca pressed on. "The Chinese&mdash;"

"Same. Raging centralists, at _huge_ scale. And they weren't doing it to their own kids."

"I don't care. Communism is never good."

This produced genuine laughter. Sarah set down her teacup. "Rebecca. Every family in this country — yours included — is a communist dictatorship. Wealth in common, food, shelter, medicine, provided to every member regardless of contribution. 'From each according to his means, to each according to his need.'"

Rebecca opened her mouth. Closed it. The logic was airtight and it had to be a trick.

Janet watched the struggle with kind eyes. "Do you tell your children to share?"

"Obviously. It's just common decency."

"Yes," Sarah said. "That's what I'm telling you. The decency of sharing is the only part of communism that matters. The rest is political bullshit and excuses to grab power, same as every other political movement."

The kitchen went quiet except for the wall clock and distant laughter from the yard. Rebecca stared into her tea.

"When we first got here," Maria said, "Miguel and I kept waiting for the catch. Took months before I understood: _the catch is that when nobody can make you return a favour, you'd have to be a total jerk not to._ And generosity goes to the deserving _because_ it's optional."

"Your father was afraid of totalitarian states that used communist rhetoric to justify tyranny," Sarah said softly. "He was right to fear it. But what he was really afraid of was his children being taken from him and controlled by strangers. 

You probably noticed we do childcare as a sort of group thing. I can't be everywhere, but _we_ can. It takes a village, y'know. Your dad didn't want his kids controlled by strangers. So don't be a stranger."

Through the window Rebecca could see her eight-year-old son teaching the local kids a card game on the front lawn, all of them sharing a pile of biscuits someone's mother had sent along.

"The funny thing," she said finally, "is that this feels more like the America my grandfather thought he was coming to than anything I ever experienced in Brisbane."

"I don't think that place ever existed," Janet said. "It was a PR lie. Send us your tired, your poor, your huddled masses ... so we can put them in sweatshops and tell them it's their own fault."

Rebecca sighed, deflating. She looked out the window at her boy.

"He's happy. That's all Miklos wanted."

---

A knock at the back door interrupted the moment. Janet called out "Come in!" and Tom Bradley appeared, shouldering an enormous wooden crate that looked like it weighed as much as he did.

"Ladies, I've got a problem," he announced, setting it down with a thud that rattled the teacups. "My mate Dave up in Lismore had a tree come down in his orchard. Insurance won't cover the fruit loss, but the tree was loaded. He sent down whatever he could salvage before it spoiled."

The crate was packed solid with citrus — oranges, lemons, limes, grapefruits, and several varieties Rebecca couldn't name.

"Good Lord, Tom," Janet said. "There must be twenty kilos in there."

"Sixty. Two more in the ute." He looked hopefully around the table. "Anyone interested?"

Rebecca watched, baffled, as each woman declined in turn.

"Oh, I couldn't — we've still got fruit from last week's swap," Janet started.

"Miguel just brought home a bag from the Kowalskis yesterday," Maria waved her hand.

"The kids are barely keeping up with what's in our bowl already," Sarah added.

Rebecca found herself about to follow suit, then caught herself. "But... it's free fruit. Good fruit, by the look of it."

"What would I do with sixty kilogrammes of oranges? They'd be mouldy before we got halfway through."

Maria picked up a grapefruit and turned it over in her hands. "Now, if someone wanted to make marmalade..." She raised her eyebrows at the table.

What happened next took about forty seconds. Sarah remembered her grandmother's recipe. Janet offered lemon curd. Maria pointed out the cafeteria kitchen had the big preserving pots. A notepad appeared from a drawer. Names were being written down.

"The Patel family has the good scales," Maria said.

"And the Williams owe me a favour after those preserves," Janet added. "They can bring the cream for the scones."

Rebecca looked from face to face. "Who gets to keep the jam?"

The planning stopped. Four faces turned toward her with the same expression — polite, gentle confusion.

"Everyone who wants some," Tom said, as if explaining something a child had asked. "That's the whole point."

"But someone's providing the fruit, someone's kitchen, someone's labour—"

"And someone'll provide jars, someone else the sugar, and the cafeteria's going to find out it's providing the space," Sarah said. "By the time we're done we'll have made more than any one family could use. Everyone takes home what they need, and the surplus goes on the community shelf for winter."

Tom hefted the crate. "I'll drop this at the caf and let people know we're doing preserves on Sunday." He headed for the door.

"Tom," Rebecca called after him. "What do you get out of it? You could have sold it."

He paused, genuinely puzzled. "What would I want with sixty kilos of citrus? I like marmalade, but not _that_ much." He grinned. "Besides — jam-day! You'll see Sunday. I wouldn't miss it. I always go home with something I wouldn't have thought of making."

The door closed and Rebecca stared at the empty space where the crate had been. Back home, sixty kilograms of free citrus would have been a windfall to hoard or carefully ration. She'd also been _given_ a huge bottle of pasta sauce last week. The provenance was suddenly, obviously clear. Small guilt.

"That wasn't a show," she said finally. "You really are like this."

"Tom's fruit is Tom's fruit," Janet said. "No one has any right to it. He could let it rot in his shed if he wanted. He could make fifty jars of marmalade and eat them over the next decade." She smiled. "How much marmalade does a man need?"

A radio in a charging cradle demanded attention: _beeedunk!_ Janet picked it up. "Hendersons, over."

Forest's voice crackled through. "I hear we have a citrus glut."

"We're sorting it."

"Good-oh. I was just talking to Oleksiy. If you can spare ten or twenty kilos — how do you feel about us having a crack at some Cointreau?"

Maria was on her feet, fist in the air. "Pick 'em up at the caf!"

Sarah and Janet stared at her, faces blank.

"What?"

"You are so predictable." They grinned.

---

Jam-day was not what Rebecca expected.

She'd imagined something earnest and organised, perhaps with a coordinator and a sign-up sheet. Instead the cafeteria filled up gradually with whoever turned up, found something that needed doing, and did it. Mrs. Chen arrived wanting to learn jam-making and left three hours later, mission accomplished. The Williams family brought cream and went home with marmalade. Children appeared, ate biscuits, played in the yard, were absorbed briefly into the peeling and stirring, and escaped again. At some point a speaker appeared on a shelf and someone put on music.

Rebecca found herself elbow-deep in grapefruit peel with a woman she'd only met once before, discussing school scheduling. She hadn't introduced herself or asked permission to join. She'd just started helping, and that had apparently been the right thing to do.

At the far end of the cafeteria, Forest and Oleksiy had commandeered a corner and surrounded themselves with a mysterious assembly of glass jars, copper tubing, and what looked like a laboratory hotplate. The discussion around it was animated and technical and occasionally involved the phrase "we should probably not do that." Des was there, listening with the expression of a man witnessing something wonderfully inadvisable.

By mid-afternoon, every pot will filled, sealed with, of all things, beeswax. They were racked, packed stacked and jacked. People helped themselves. Nobody was greedy. Rebecca counted the jars she was taking home: fourteen. She had contributed two hours of work, half a bag of sugar, and a dozen empty jars she'd been about to throw out. She'd started out trying to work out what was fair, and realised she didn't _want_ that much jam. Plus it was fun.

"Still waiting for the invoice?" Maria appeared beside her, carrying a box of her own.

Rebecca laughed, properly. "Maybe I've been doing the maths wrong."

"Life is for living, not for keeping score."

Monday morning in the maintenance yard Des and Jake Chen were elbow-deep in the guts of Ground Team B's primary excavator, the smell of diesel and grease heavy in the still air.

"Pass me the seventeen, would you?"

Jake handed over the wrench. "How's Bec settling in after yesterday?"

"Bugger, it's an eighteen." Des swapped sockets. "She's come around a bit. Still has questions though."

"Such as?"

"Whether there's a catch. Whether someone presents a bill eventually." Des emerged with the hydraulic assembly and set it on the workbench. "There we go. Better than new, actually — that hose isn't rated for the pressure."

"Probably put in to save money on a proper mechanic," Jake observed. "Funny how that works."

Des wiped his hands. "Tell me honestly — is there a catch?"

"The catch is it's a small place. Everyone knows everyone. Being a jerk catches up," Jake said. "People remember." He pulled a torque wrench from the cabinet, checked the calibration out of habit. "Also Forest has been known to have quiet words with people who consistently take without giving. Apparently it involves a lot of swearing and pointed questions about whether they're practicing to be politicians."

"So social pressure."

"Same thing that holds everywhere together everywhere. Except here it happens to your face, where you can answer back. You're not stuck paying taxes to fund some arsehole's decisions about your life from a city three states away." Jake handed Des a fresh hose. "Besides — look at this afternoon. Neither of us owns this excavator. Nobody asked us to fix it. We're just doing it because it needs doing and we can."

Des considered that, fitting the new hose to the assembly. "Rebecca asked me last night whether we're staying."

"What'd you say?"

"I said I worked harder in the last three weeks than I have since I was a brickie's labourer, but half the time I feel like I'm on holidays. The pay ought to be shit but we never spend any money so it's more than we need. Our kid plays outside until dark without us watching every shadow." He torqued the fitting down. "If this is communism, the Stalinists were doing it wrong."

"They were doing centralisation," Jake said. "Top-down, enforced, by strangers who don't know you and don't care about you. Soon as you do that, you've killed what you were claiming to organise." He laughed: "Forest says that so often that Sarah threatens to put it on a t-shirt."

They continued in comfortable silence. A kookaburra laughed from somewhere in the trees, and some magpie turned up, warbling.

"There's something I should probably tell you," Jake said, returning the torque wrench to its drawer. "The reason it's always 'Forest says' and 'Forest thinks' — it's not just the Vanguard thing, though that's part of it. It's also who owns the valley. Forest, Oleksiy, Brian, Chris, Janna, and Gerry."

"Right, makes sense." Des paused. "Wait. Gerry?"

"Two hundred and forty acres. Two of the lots. Sophia leases the admin compound from him."

Des straightened up slowly. "The gardener."

"He has the tools, the compound needs doing. Man's not interested in appearances. He's very interested in the valley working."

"The _gardener_ is a major stakeholder."

"We're all major stakeholders, we live here." Jake grinned. "Besides — this is the ground team. We're armed to the teeth. Who could possibly _make_ us do anything? Forest goes out of his way to get buy-in because that's the only kind that means anything."

Des was quiet as they locked up the shed and crossed the absurdly well-groomed compound lawn toward the residential end of the valley.

"You know what I keep noticing?" he said eventually. "Back in Brisbane I'd have spent this afternoon calculating whether we could afford to fix that thing properly or had to bodge it. Here I just... fixed it. With good parts."

"Weird how not justifying every decision to an accountant makes work more satisfying."

"Almost like the man knew what he was doing."

"Don't tell him that. His head's big enough."

"Is there anything he doesn't have an opinion on?"

Jake considered. "Pineapple on pizza. Apparently he's diplomatically neutral."

"Sensible position."

They walked on through the late afternoon, toward the smell of dinner and the sound of children winding down.
