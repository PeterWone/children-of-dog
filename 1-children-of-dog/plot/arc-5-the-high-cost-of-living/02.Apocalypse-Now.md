The chapter was named Ragnarök. It was renamed because that was ostentatious. "Apocalypse Now" is the reference that replaced it: both the Coppola film — the long journey upriver to find the man who has become something beyond law — and the literal Greek meaning, "unveiling." What is unveiled here is the shape of the world after the gods have acted.

The coordinated strike happens in sequence, not simultaneously, and the sequence is the message. First the Port of Brisbane — a kinetic weapon, rods from god, Mach-10 tungsten bolts from orbit, the BP holding tank, everything burning. Emergency services converge exactly as expected. Then, once assets are committed and in position, every exit is cut at once: roads, rail lines, airport runways, harbour crossings, gone. Then power — tiny drones rising from fields and gutters like moths, strapping transmission towers with silicon bandages and sparkling fuses, the towers leaning and falling in sequence. Then the water main, cut in four places, precisely chosen for maximum inaccessibility. Sydney and Melbourne hit in the same hour. Senior Sergeant Kath Morrison watches her operations board go solid red and deduces it immediately: not terrorism. No demands. The point of terrorism is to frighten people into submission. There were no demands because submission is not the offer being made. This is war. By the end of the chapter she revises even that: genocide.

Morrison gets a helicopter and a priority fuel chit, ostensibly to arrest the vandal with the drones. Eighty kilometres out, Autumn challenges them on the radio and reframes the mission entirely: Forest died two years ago, they don't do surrender, but they do do search and rescue. She gives them a turn-point. The valley fits the police helicopter with long-range fuel pods, a firmware update, a sat-phone, and a mission list with GPS coordinates. The police pilot, Phillip, is comprehensively outclassed at every step and knows it. He goes anyway. A week later the commissioner, maskless because disease is rife and there is no water to wash, takes off his cap and delivers the chapter's epitaph: Genghis Khan. Relatively speaking, almost no casualties in the attack itself. Two million people who cannot be fed or kept clean or kept apart. "This is how the world ends; not with a bang, but a whimper."

The Ragnarök parallel is structural. In the myth, Ragnarök is not a villain's triumph — it is the necessary end of a corrupt age, the destruction that precedes renewal. The gods themselves fall. The world is remade. This chapter is the destruction of the old order not by invasion but by the withdrawal of its own foundations: cut the exits, cut the power, cut the water, and the leviathan kills itself. The commissioner understands this. The man who was trying to enforce business as usual until the last moment ends by quoting Eliot and whispering to no one. What the valley did was not cruel by accident. It was the most precisely calibrated possible application of consequence.

The title's second meaning is the film. Willard goes upriver to find Kurtz, who has gone beyond the law and become something the army can neither command nor allow to exist. The parallel is inverted: here it is the institutions of law that have become the thing that cannot be allowed to exist, and the figure they sent upriver to arrest is already running search and rescue missions with their own helicopter.
