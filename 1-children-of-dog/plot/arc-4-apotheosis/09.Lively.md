Karaoke night in the longhouse. The valley has descended into joyful chaos: Marcus and Jenny's kids learning a suspiciously martial dance that is obviously Bruce's influence, the Henderson twins arguing over song choices, Shorty losing an argument with his daughter while she records him for posterity. The mead is winning against Sophia's nanites, third stein and still upright. Oleksiy has done his set — Ukrainian folk songs that had half the valley weeping and the other half dancing — and is now across the room with his arm around Sissi, an arrangement Sophia had apparently missed happening.

Maria drags Sophia back to the table-stage for a second set, having selected the song herself. The song is Venus. The opening synth drops and Sophia tries for dignity while standing on furniture. Autumn's presence drone responds to each verse: when Sophia sings "Black as the dark night she was," it dims the lights until her black clothes absorb what illumination remains. The AI is stage-managing the symbolism, making truth visible. Maria sings with unfiltered joy and entirely unnecessary hip movements. Old Bill from the north ridge goes from stern to cracking into laughter and raises his beer. Bruce climbs onto the table wearing a tutu over his combat frame and flanks Sophia like honour guard at a festival. The timber groans but holds — six inches thick, built for worse. The longhouse hangs between sacrament and farce.

Sophia finds herself actually meaning the bridge: "Just don't forget that the price is yours to pay." Three years of fieldwork. Propaganda. Steering unwilling people toward an open door. A price she would pay again. The faces watching are not mocking. They are celebrating her, and the impossible thing they have become together.

Afterward, half-sobered, Maria delivers the chapter's thesis in words that are deliberately ambiguous: "They must see you like this, human. Happy. Lively. Not just black armour and space guns." Human can be read as an adjective — relatable, approachable, one of them. It can equally be read as a noun of address, in which case Maria is speaking to someone she does not consider entirely human anymore, reminding the Valkyrie what she must appear to be. Then something shifts. Something ancient and implacable stares out of Maria's eyes for a moment. "You will serve, Valkyrie," she says, in a voice with an odd timbre, the words falling like hammers. Then she blinks and Maria is grinning and herself again. Sophia notes there is quite a lot of Maria for such a small person, very carefully stacked — "divinely arranged," she thinks, and immediately wishes she hadn't. Autumn adds, dryly, that the footage is excellent. Fabulous propaganda. Truth with guns and trumpets and eighties synth-pop. And, most importantly, tits.

Lulu arrives by flinging her armour through the door ahead of her — chest plate, boots, helmet bouncing across the floor — and announcing herself to the room: "Hey bitches! Didja miss me?" Maria cues up "The Look" as Lulu strides across the longhouse. The lyrics match her movement precisely — walking like a man, hitting like a hammer — and she may have leaned into it. She vaults onto the table, owns the space through the first verse, then drops the mic and lets Tictac take over the sound system for "Shoot to Thrill" and "Dirty Deeds." She earned it in blood and lightning, and the room knows it. For a moment, in the lights, she is limned, brandishing a spear, and then it is just the mic stand. Maybe.

Oleksiy settles beside Sophia and offers the chapter's other thesis in five words: "Worry with friends is less." The longhouse rolls on around them — Tom's eldest brave and off-key, the Henderson twins preparing chaotic duet, Bill telling a story three people have heard before and don't mind. Bruce acquires a beer and explains to Eric exactly what he plans to do with it: wave it around and spill it while singing. His voice has none of its threatening harmonics. It overflows with bonhomie.

Sophia raises her beer to Forest's empty chair. The old bastard would have loved this.

The title is the word Maria uses: "Lively. Not just black armour and space guns." The arc is apotheosis, and apotheosis means elevation — but you cannot be elevated if you forget how to be human first. This chapter is the moment before the hard work resumes, and it is doing necessary work of its own. Autumn records it all because the footage is real and true precisely because it is unguarded. The closing image earns its plainness: gods walked among them, singing and dancing, eating and drinking, and they were themselves, as mountains are hilly. Being themselves is the whole point. The divine presence in the chapter is not metaphorical decoration — the novel has been careful about this since the Homework chapter. Something spoke through Maria. The Valkyrie was told she will serve.

The pantheon is assembling. Forest is Odin (named in Homework). Autumn is Loki (named by the children — something wild and potent in her eyes that they'd never seen). Lulu is Hervor, the berserker. Maria is Freyja. Freyja is the goddess of love, beauty, and fertility — and also of war and death. She commands the Valkyries and takes half the slain; Odin takes the other half. Friday is her day, named for her as the Norse cognate of Venus across mythologies (vendredi — Venus's day in French). Maria chose the song Venus. The "ancient and implacable" thing that stares out of her eyes and commissions Sophia is not Odin again — it is the Valkyrie-commander confirming the appointment directly. Forest elevated Sophia; Freyja ratifies it. The novel signals this in plain sight: "quite a lot of Maria for such a small person, very carefully stacked — divinely arranged," which presents itself as a joke about Shorty's domestic situation and is not.
