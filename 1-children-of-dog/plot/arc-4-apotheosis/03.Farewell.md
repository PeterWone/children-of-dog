Sophia descends from the Galya carrying something that is the wrong shape and too small, and she is moving too slowly. She lays what remains of Forest on Oleksiy's workbench without a word, and turns to let her tears explain. Oleksiy takes two steps and puts his arms around her. She almost crushes him with her amplified strength and backs off, ashamed. He answers her unasked question: she knows why. She untangles Forest from the remains of the jacket herself — separating man from myth, as the summary puts it — and asks Autumn to make things less gruesome for Trixie but not to clean the jacket, and not to fix the missing eye. She does not explain why.

That night they build a pyre on the level pad outside his workshop, surrounded by his half-finished projects. Two women light it with torches. The ground teams stand witness in full kit, a living amphitheatre. Darkness falls and the fireflies in their thousands join the sparks. They retreat to the longhouse, where the lads from Charlie team unwrap the lamb already on the spit, and a quiet unvoiced toast is raised.

Then a young man says he can't believe Forest isn't here. Oleksiy lifts him by his lumberjacket onto a stool by the fire, pours him mead, carves him meat, and says: "I see him clearly." His friends make him be Forest for the evening. He tells the story of the dog — the pup that growled at Forest over a bone too big for its jaws, was batted aside, and woke him from a nap under a soursop tree by pushing a piece of the broken bone into his mouth. When he finishes, another lad rises and speaks of cane harvesters and corporate folly. They remember a man's life in his own voice.

In a corner, Trixie and Sophia drink a robust red wine that neither of them likes. "This stuff is bloody awful. I never understood why he kept bringing it home." Autumn, who has finally arrived with her presence drone, reveals that Forest asked her to take Sophia on as a Vanguard in her own right — something that has never happened before, but there is no rule against it. Class II armour out of the gate, years of experience, zero points to start. Trixie laughs, grimly: "He's dead and he's still trying to change the world."

A child approaches the fire and asks where Forest is. Oleksiy, on the back foot for one frozen heartbeat, spreads his arms wide: "He is gone, and now he is everywhere!" Sophia thumps her chest and barks "Here!" with a vehemence that surprises even her. They abandon the red wine for a late harvest wine — thick, golden, sweet, also Forest's — and toast him: "You're not getting away that easily, you old bastard. I'll see you in hell."

Morning. Sophia activates her nanites to deal with the hangover, which she reflects is the best kind of cheating. Eric has already set up the absurd boiler espresso machine he and Forest built from a pressure cooker and a group head assembly — a contraption that by all rights should produce bitter, burnt coffee but produces sublime coffee on demand over an open fire, because they had so much faith in their own ability they collapsed the quantum wave into the only universe that mattered. He gives a mug to Trixie, who sheds a tear she doesn't explain. The wrong person gave it to her.

Trixie mentions she's asked Janna for beetroots — sugar beet instead of cane, full circle, you harvest it with a knife. Sophia wonders whether she'll ever think like that about the needs of her people. Autumn tells her to quit second-guessing herself: "Just be, O child of dog." Sophia sinks into the warmth of the rekindled fire and, far beneath the surface, feels the grief for what it is — not banked coals but magma, wild, ungovernable, ready to cleave the earth and blacken the skies, unflinching of the cost.

The arc is called Apotheosis — the elevation of a mortal to divine status — and this chapter is the moment it happens. Both of them. Forest is elevated by the community without anyone deciding to do it: they make a young man speak his stories in his voice, Oleksiy says "I see him clearly" as fact not consolation, a child asks where he is and gets two simultaneous answers — "everywhere" and a chest-thump of "here." He has stopped being a person and become a pattern that lives in the people he shaped. His apotheosis is communal, immediate, and entirely out of anyone's hands.

Sophia's is quieter and handed to her deliberately. Forest arranged it. She is named Vanguard — the first to be elevated by another rather than by crisis — and she receives it in the middle of grief, without ceremony, almost as an aside. She doesn't feel ready. She wonders whether she'll ever fill his boots. Autumn tells her to stop second-guessing and just be. The magma at the end is the answer: she already is. The question was never capability.

The Homework chapter named Forest as Odin. Here Odin, from beyond the pyre, has made Sophia his Valkyrie — the one who carries the chosen from the field of battle and into whatever comes next. He chose her. The mythic register the children were debating in class has become the literal structure of events.

Everything in the chapter carries his absence as a kind of presence. The coffee machine that shouldn't work but does because he and Eric believed it would. Trixie's tear when the wrong person hands her a cup. The red wine nobody liked but he kept bringing home. The beetroot idea, which is Trixie thinking in exactly the way he taught — systems, full circle, harvest it with a knife. Sophia wonders whether she will ever think like that, then catches herself: he gave the boots to her. To her specifically.

The chapter's gift — Sophia named as Vanguard — is Forest's final act of goading. He is still trying to change the world from beyond the fire. The magma image at the end is not despair; it is the chapter refusing to resolve grief into something manageable. What Sophia carries forward is not sadness. It is force.
