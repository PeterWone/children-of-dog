Lulu's augmentation, experienced from the inside. Her consent was as informed as Tictac could make it, and heartbreakingly simple: "It will help me survive. Do it." She said it the way someone says something they have already decided.

The process runs through three dream stages that mirror the procedure's physical reality. In the first, she follows a hooded figure with a lantern through mist and up a mountain, arriving at a snow-blasted plateau where he attacks her with a staff — slow at first, then faster, faster, until they are moving beyond human possibility. He is not an enemy; he is a teacher, and the lesson is that skill without a body to embody it is nothing. She is given a staff and learns to stop dodging and start meeting him. The combat is a mythic version of what the nanomachines are doing to her nervous system: rewriting it for war. In the second stage she defends a walled summit against waves of antithesis with a rifle, then a pistol, then close quarters — each weapon escalating as the creatures evolve, all while her bones ache with wrongness she cannot name. This is the skeletal scaffold stage; her bones are soft, and she can feel it in her dream as grinding, shattering agony she simply refuses to stop for. In the third stage the creatures become geometric abstractions and the weapons shed physical form entirely — she is fighting in a space that doesn't exist with tools that have no names. This is the neural restructuring: new pathways, new reflex architectures, being threaded through a brain still growing.

Between the dream sequences she wakes, briefly, into white and pain and Tictac's careful voice. He tells her the bones are decalcifying, the scaffold is in, the re-ossification is complete: one hundred fifty-three centimetres, forty percent more muscle density, sixty percent more bone density, enhanced proprioception. The numbers mean nothing to her. Breathing again is something.

When she fully wakes she can see Tictac — he has a face now, which she accepts without a second thought because Tictac knows everything and if he wants a face he can certainly have one. She is immobile in the pod's motor suppression, learning she is much stronger than yesterday when she flings both arms up and nearly breaks the pod. She is told she must rest. She asks to go back to the mountain with the rifle. She found it fun and she's pretty sure she can do better on target queuing if she uses burst fire discipline from the start. Tictac, exercising considerable digital self-control, sets up another scenario.

The epigraph is Bon Scott: "It's a long way to the top if you wanna rock'n'roll." The mist parts again to power chords and wailing bagpipes. Lulu grins, hefts her rifle, and begins to climb.

The chapter is a hero's initiation written for someone who is ten years old and completely unfazed by it. The mythic framework — the cloaked guide, the mountain trial, the gift of a weapon — is classical, but the candidate is a child who spent the agony of bone-melting in a combat simulation because she wanted to practice her three-split overmind. The gap between the ancient form and the particular person filling it is both funny and quietly staggering.
