Forest helps three valley kids with Norse mythology homework while fixing a bench hinge. He treats the mythology as meaningful metaphor rather than quaint legend. Odin is anyone who sacrificed comfortable certainty for harsh reality — career, reputation, social standing — to understand something that matters more. The Norns are pattern recognition given divine form; they don't know the future, they understand social networks well enough to make certain outcomes inevitable. The Range Mothers, he suggests with genuine seriousness, are exactly this. Autumn says "Here we go," with the resignation of someone who knows where this is heading.

Berserkers were not magic warriors; they were people who had trained so hard and committed so completely that fear stopped being relevant. He offers Lulu as an example: eleven years old now, seven when she was found fighting antithesis in rubble without even being a Vanguard. The Valkyries choosing the slain is just the necessity of deciding who lives and who dies in combat — not romantic, not magical, just the thing that tears you apart if you let yourself think about it. The Norse built Ragnarök into their worldview so that when disaster came they would face it head-on rather than be surprised by it. The valley is doing the same thing. Jackson says, quietly: "Because it's what you do." The homework session dissolves briefly into Monty Python's "Always Look on the Bright Side of Death" before returning to the point: doom isn't the end, it's a threshold. Something different on the other side, but you have to go through the fire to get there.

Autumn notes the kids have figured something out before Forest has. He doesn't ask what. He closes his toolbox and sends them to their homework.

Afterwards, Trixie finds him sitting on the bench staring at nothing. He has noticed he is teaching children about mythological patterns by acting out the mythological pattern of the wise wanderer who teaches children about patterns — turtles all the way down. He knows what happens to the hermit who stands against the falling empire. Trixie tells him she has read his old mythology books, and names him Odin directly: "You may be surprised to know I read those books." She reminds him that Odin's children survive, that Thor's daughter inherits Mjölnir, that the world burns but something grows from the ashes. He isn't doing this because he thinks he'll see it. He's doing it to make sure someone does. He goes to do the dishes.

The next day, the schoolyard debate about which valley adult is which Norse figure is fierce and only half joking. Oleksiy is definitely Thor. The liveliest debate is whether Autumn is Loki. Some kids ask her directly. Her avatar shifts between masculine and feminine, wild and potent, something in her eyes they have never seen before, and she asks them — in a tone that is not at all reassuring — whether that is what they want. Four of them have an existential crisis. Forest makes her fix it, threatening "Friends of Loki with Mickey Mouse ears" if she doesn't. She does. The banter between them about stripper poles and whose idea it was ends the chapter on a note of comfortable, combative affection.
