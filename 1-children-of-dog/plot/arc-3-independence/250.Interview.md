Lulu and Bruce's scenic railway trip to the Blue Mountains is redirected when Tictac reports antithesis establishing a hive in the western suburbs with insufficient state response. They go help. Twenty minutes and one very satisfying explosion later, Lulu delivers an after-action report to a colonel who is taking notes with an actual pen, because her ten-year-old voice makes people treat her like a school presentation. She formally transfers a command drone and charging base to the colonel's forces, Bruce says "accept the gift, say thank you," and the colonel says thank you immediately. Smart man.

Tictac steers her through the field hospital for optics and genuine need — she hands out inhalers to the worst cases, tells a soldier his intestines will be annoying for six hours while they regrow but he'll live, and moves on. When reporters ambush her in the car park, Bruce declines to do journalism on a hospital loading dock and sends them booking information.

The chapter is structured as the interview itself interspersed with reaction streams, professional analysis, and commentary, each medium trying to process what it is watching. The interview is conducted by Lisa Honeywell and goes badly for Lisa from the first exchange. Lulu says violence is nearly always the answer and if it doesn't work you didn't use enough. She argues she is a sovereign power, on the grounds that sovereignty means either nobody can stop you or nobody will because the consequences are too much. A law professor analyses this live and concludes she is technically not wrong by any established definition, which is the most disturbing part of his analysis. She says she no longer needs sleep — one of her six parallel instances handles dreams and emotional processing. A psychologist stops her recording to fact-check whether the Class III Medical catalogue actually supports voluntary induced and medically stabilised multi-stream consciousness. It does.

The chapter's pivot: Lisa pushes Lulu on remorse, fear, the theft of her childhood. Lulu says Chloe died a long time ago, hiding under a table because she was scared. Good riddance — she was weak and couldn't protect anyone. A streaming reactor freezes the video: "She's talking about herself. Chloe IS — WAS — her name. She just killed herself. Not metaphorically." He does not end stream. They all keep watching.

Midway through the interview, an assassination attempt. A bullet punches through Lulu's temple. The studio erupts. Lulu's voice comes from her armour speakers, tinny and annoyed: she moved her brain out of there ages ago, things trying to kill her is normal, and she is furious about the silk — third time, period dyes, impossible to get blood out, they're never going to rent to her again. Bruce inspects the hole in her head like checking oil levels and administers a haemo-restore while she sways and complains about the eight thousand points she had to spend last time this happened instead of a bone marrow upgrade.

Bruce, when finally asked to introduce himself, steps out of the shadows uncostumed — Lulu notices this and understands immediately — and says in a voice with the harmonics of a choir: "I am Bruce and we are legion." Then it coalesces to a single baritone: "If I were going to harm you I would have already done it." An ethics professor, reviewing the full transcript afterwards, finds she cannot identify a logical flaw in Lulu's consequentialist framework by any ethical system she applies. She stops recording without conclusion. The tactical analyst who opened the chapter with professional enthusiasm ends his series: "I can explain the tactics, but I don't want to."

The chapter is the closing beat of arc 3: Independence. What arc 3 has been building — the valley's refusal to depend on institutions, the deliberate cultivation of self-sufficient people — arrives here in its most concentrated form. Lulu is independence made flesh, the kind that cannot be revoked because it lives in the person rather than in any charter or permission. The interview format is the old world attempting to make her legible and failing completely. Every professional who tries — ethicist, psychologist, lawyer, military analyst — runs their framework and either finds it doesn't apply or finds that she is technically correct, which is worse. The title trails off for a reason: "Interview with the, um..." The world cannot complete the sentence.

The Fitzgerald epigraph is the key: "The test of a first-rate intelligence is the ability to hold two opposed ideas in the mind at the same time, and still retain the ability to function." Lulu holds six minds at once. She is the literal fulfillment of the metaphor, which is also the joke, which is also the horror. The "Chloe died" moment is the chapter's dark centre: she did not grow up, she killed a version of herself and chose what replaced it. The world finds this monstrous. Lulu finds it obvious. The gap between those two responses is what the chapter is about.
