Rifle fire from the range is just background noise now — the mothers barely look up from their notes. They are planning textile production for three hundred people without industrial supply chains. Jenny has been deep in the history of English textile mills: horrifying working conditions, but brilliant machinery. The thread-count mathematics eventually hits the room like a bucket of water: to clothe three hundred people with ongoing replacement, they need millions of kilometres of thread per year. "No wonder they built a machine."

Priya asks whether they should — the question is real, and nobody answers immediately. But eventually the argument resolves: exactly as complicated as it needs to be and no more. Valley-growable fibres: flax, hemp, wool, maybe alpaca. Oleksiy mentions that his grandmother spun and wove everything for her family, and adds quietly: "Who controls cloth controls life."

Rebecca produces a pair of cheap Chinese shorts — the cut bizarre, Eric confirms this in terms that leave no doubt — but draws out the drawstring to show what it is: cotton cord with elasticity built entirely into the weave structure, no synthetics. Susan examines it and is impressed. Eloise wanders in, glances at it, says "ribbon plait — a five-strand flat braid of tube braids will have significant crush," and turns away to look for Autumn. Rebecca stares at her with her mouth open. Forest, overhearing, has a back-paddock epiphany about rope: the same braid principle, scaled up, solves the rope problem first and they can refine it down to elastic cord from there.

Forest joins properly and explains the Spinning Jenny — James Hargreaves, 1764, eight spindles from one wheel, still human-powered, all parts valley-buildable. He and Oleksiy could prototype one over a weekend. Autumn has already dropped the plans in the shared folder. Then Autumn goes further: the Jacquard loom, 1804, reads punch cards, produces any pattern you can encode. Charles Babbage saw one and it gave him the Analytical Engine. Ada Lovelace wrote about it. The punch card is the first programmable machine. Teaching children to design patterns in holes is teaching them to think in algorithms, to break complex instructions into repeatable steps, to debug when something goes wrong. "You're planning centuries ahead." — "Someone has to."

Autumn's parting line as she glides out to deal with a loaded weapon left lying in the corner: "I'm afraid I have to go and loom." The rifle's owner is driven in before Oleksiy by an outraged Lisa, Scott red-faced, Sophia trailing behind with her usual bemused expression. Later Sophia tells Oleksiy she can't tell whether Lisa wants to kill him or marry him. The old Slav says nothing but smiles.
