Two highway patrol officers are dispatched to the valley following a complaint about explosions. The road defeats them progressively: pot-holes, the water crossing, and then a freshly felled pine lying precisely across the road with half a dozen young men in red-checked flannel shirts standing beside it. Three bends back, a girl and her not-quite boyfriend have already flipped a cattle grid into a spike strip and locked it in place, then vanished into the pig runs with their bows. Forest's words echo in her head as she goes: "Policing is by consent, although the police seem to have forgotten that."

Jack, who felled the tree, confronts Senior Sergeant Kafka with preternatural self-possession — welcoming him to "my valley," questioning him in return, invoking private property, and eventually escorting the officers to the administration as though he is doing them a favour. Kafka cannot dominate the situation, cannot find the other kids, and cannot locate the script for this. Artillery day. Field exercise. Junior ground team. The valley's teenagers have been trained to handle exactly this situation, and they are enjoying it.

Then Sophia arrives. She comes in aboard the Galya, which hovers at fifty metres, and rappels out without clipping on — she snagged the rope in one hand, twirled the bottom around her leg, and fell. She lands in a dramatic crouch, one hand on the ground. Jack rolls his eyes: "Do you know how long I'd have to listen to dad lecturing me if he saw me do that?" She grins at him. The frustrated bureaucrat who spent her early months in the valley looking for leverage she could never find has become someone who shows off for teenagers and enjoys it. She refers to Autumn as "the spirit of our little forest" — the word *our* is not accidental. She issues the clearances, listens to the story, and sends Kafka home with coffee so good he will remember it. The Baldwin epigraph says the rest: freedom is something people take.
