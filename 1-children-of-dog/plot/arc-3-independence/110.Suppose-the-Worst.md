Ken and Shorty sit on deck chairs on the roof-terrace of Ken's cottage — half-metre granite walls, insulated cavity, built into the hillside — watching Ken's boy lying on a log over the low dam, fishing for blueclaw with a net and a bit of gristle. Ken looks at his son and, for the first time in Shorty's memory, smiles without hesitation. Shorty asks the question that has been hanging between them: "What if it doesn't work?" Ken answers without flinching — then they will die on their feet for their own reasons, and until then, the boy will be happy.

The conversation turns to subsistence: Shorty worries that no money and growing everything yourself is just medieval peasant life. Ken disagrees. They argue about building supplies and what the valley actually still depends on. The argument is interrupted when they drift down to the longhouse, where half the valley has assembled — range mothers making preserves, Oleksiy and Forest turning a lamb over the firepit.

In the middle of it all: Eric and Sophia are bent over a diagram. She is planning a home. Not accommodation, not a billet — a home, in the valley, with timber foundations and stone walls and a view. Their debate grows animated and Autumn's presence drone renders the proposed sites in lilac, shifting with each objection. Forest gets drawn in on the question of how to make stringybark last in the ground; he becomes genuinely excited working through charring, soaking in turps, tarpaint, the chemistry of rot — "there's never time to do it right, but there's always time to do it again." Sophia is not in a rush. She likes the smell of linseed oil. It reminds her of her grandmother's house.

Later she sits back with a cup of the valley's mead, which gets easier to drink every year, and asks Autumn why she is always lilac. Autumn answers with Ingrid Bergman sepia, then Cortana's wink, then lilac motes fading to darkness. Eric taps his mug against Sophia's and they drink — to Autumn's wisdom, to the fallen, to Forest's sublime madness, and to the honour of stewardship. Around them the room falls quiet and many glasses rise. The Greek epigraph has become literal: she is planting trees.
